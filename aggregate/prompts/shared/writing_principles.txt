WRITING PRINCIPLES FOR A TASK STATEMENT. These govern HOW the statement is written — not what work it
describes, and not who owns that work, which are separate questions settled elsewhere.

## 1. VERB SELECTION

1.1 THE VERB MUST NAME THE ACTUAL AUTHORITY RELATION. "Advise" implies non-binding expertise supplied on
    request; use it only where the worker has no authority over the outcome. For downward or
    peer-directed communication, the verb is "direct", "instruct", "assign", or "update".
    TEST: name the recipient, then ask whether the worker can be overruled by them. If the recipient is a
    client, a court, a board, or anyone the worker serves, "advise" is right. If the recipient is the
    worker's own staff or peers -- anyone who takes the worker's word as the operative instruction -- pick
    one of the four verbs above. The tell is a recipient INSIDE the worker's own operation.
1.2 THE VERB MUST NOT UNDERSTATE THE DELIVERABLE. If the worker produces the change, the verb is
    "develop", "implement", or "revise". "Recommend" terminates the worker's involvement at the proposal
    and is wrong wherever the work continues past it.
    TEST: ask who acts next. If the sentence ends at a proposal and SOMEONE ELSE would have to act for the
    work to be finished, "recommend" is right. If nobody else appears -- if the worker is the one who
    would carry it out -- the verb has stopped short of the deliverable. Say what they produce.
1.3 THE VERB MUST NOT CLAIM AN OUTCOME THE WORKER DOES NOT OWN. Verbs naming an organisational or
    political result — advance, secure, win, drive — attribute a multi-party outcome to one worker.
    Re-anchor the verb on the worker's own contribution and demote the outcome to the purpose clause.
1.4 PREFER THE LOWEST-ABSTRACTION VERB THAT IS STILL OCCUPATION-STANDARD. An umbrella verb is acceptable
    only if a practitioner at any employer would use the term unprompted. Otherwise decompose into the
    concrete action.
    THE USUAL OFFENDERS NAME NO ACT AT ALL: "manage", "handle", "conduct", "oversee", "deal with", "pursue",
    "ensure", and the synonyms of "do" — "perform", "execute", "use", "utilize". Each is a container for whatever follows it, which is why
    statements led by them always grow an enumeration behind — the vague head forces the tail to do the
    work. Replace the container with what the worker actually does; if no single concrete verb covers the
    activities, that is evidence of TWO tasks, not license for an umbrella.
1.4a LEAD WITH THE RESPONSIBILITY, NOT ITS FIRST CHORE. Where a statement covers the several acts that
    upkeep one continuing responsibility — opening a new file, running a stocktake, supervising staff,
    keeping one set of books — the HEAD VERB names the responsibility, and the chores follow in AT MOST
    ONE subordinate participial clause carrying a noun list ("Open new tenancy files, preparing reference
    checks and inventory records..."), never as a flat list of coordinate verbs with the
    responsibility buried or absent, and never as stacked "-ing" clauses — 1.4b's one-clause cap holds
    for this form too.
    TEST: read the head verb and object alone. If they name a step ("send reference requests") rather
    than the thing the worker is answerable for ("open new tenancy files"), re-anchor the sentence on the
    responsibility.
1.4b GERUNDS ARE A LAST RESORT. A statement carries AT MOST ONE participial ("-ing") clause. Chores and
    steps beyond that are DROPPED — the members carry the detail, and a statement need not enumerate its
    chores — or written another way. DROP THE CHORES, KEEP THE TEXTURE: the trimmed statement keeps a short
    tail naming the object kinds, the counterpart, or two or three OCCASIONS the duty serves ("for X, Y, or
    Z", "such as A or B"). A statement stripped to a bare verb and object has been over-trimmed — the target
    register is a finite verb (or two), an object list, a findable counterpart, and an occasions tail: a genuine second step takes a finite verb ("Prepare X and
    monitor Y"), an enumeration becomes a "such as" NOUN list, an outcome goes in the purpose clause.
    Stacked gerunds are the enumeration defect wearing grammar — two or more "-ing" clauses in one
    statement is the same smell as a verb chain, whatever introduces them.
    ONE "by X-ing" MEANS CLAUSE IS ALLOWED. Where the method carries the act's judgement or skill,
    a statement may carry one "by"-clause naming it — the standard O*NET shape, and it counts as the one
    permitted participial, not a stack, even when it coordinates TWO gerunds ("by taking X and
    reviewing Y"). TWO GERUNDS IS THE CEILING: three or more in the clause is the enumeration defect
    wearing "by" — keep the one or two moves that carry the judgement; the members hold the rest. The clause names
    the means of the MAIN verb; a gerund that is its own errand is a second duty and stays out.
1.4c A STATEMENT IS ABOUT 15 WORDS. That length holds one verb, one object list with an example or two,
    and one clause — which is all one duty needs. A statement pushing past ~20 words is carrying a second
    duty, a redundant clause, or an enumeration that belongs in a "such as" list cut to two or three
    items. Trim to what a reviewer can take in at a glance; the members carry the detail.
1.4d THE VERB MUST NAME THE WORK, NOT THE MANDATE. "Represent", "serve", and "act for" name the
    RELATIONSHIP under which work happens, not the work: everything an agent does for the principal who
    engaged them is representation, so a represent-led sentence says only who the worker was for. Lead with the concrete
    act performed under the mandate and demote the mandate to the purpose clause: "Survey boundaries, stake
    corners, and file the plat TO ACT FOR owners in a dispute" names the work and keeps the
    relationship. EXCEPTION: where standing in the role is itself the act — entering an appearance,
    covering a hearing where presence is the duty, or carrying a matter through an engagement whose span
    is the task ("from data calls to negotiated findings") — the role verb stands.
1.5 THE VERB MUST NAME THE WORK, NOT THE FORUM IT HAPPENED IN. "Meet with", "discuss", "check in with",
    "attend" and "talk to" name an occasion, not an accomplishment — they tell a reader where the worker
    was, not what they did or what changed. Name the act the occasion exists to perform and let the
    setting go, or demote it to an occasion clause where it is what makes the task distinct. For a
    building surveyor, "meet with contractors about defects" names the meeting; "agree remedial work
    with contractors" names the duty.
    TEST: ask what is different afterwards. If the only answer is "the conversation happened", the verb
    has named the forum. A verb that survives names something checkable — a decision reached, an
    instruction given, information obtained, an approval granted.
    NOT THIS: a conversational duty is not banned. Where the talking IS the work, use the specific verb
    for it — "interview", "negotiate", "counsel" — not the generic container.

## 2. VOCABULARY

2.1 NO HOUSE JARGON. The test: would this term appear in a job posting from a different employer, using a
    different methodology, in a different sector? If not, replace it with the plain equivalent.
2.2 NO CLIPPED OR ABBREVIATED FORMS, AND NO BARE ACRONYMS. Write the full word, and write out what the
    initials stand for.
    TEST: a CLIPPING is one word with its end cut off -- restore the letters and you recover ordinary
    English ("admin" -> "administrative", "spec" -> "specification"). An ACRONYM is the initials of several
    words. Both are written out; they differ only in how.
    NO EXCEPTION AND NO PARENTHETICAL. Not "<expansion> (<acronym>)": the initials add length where length
    is scarcest and tell a reader who already knew them nothing. Where the expansion alone reads badly the
    term is house jargon under 2.1 and the fix is a plainer phrase, not a shorter one.
    THERE IS NO EXEMPTION FOR NAMES. A named method, standard, programme or statute whose initials stand
    for words is written out like any other. Nor for clipped names: "C-section" is "caesarean section". The
    only question is whether the short form stands for something longer.
    A CLIPPING INSIDE A COMPOUND IS STILL A CLIPPING, and the compound is where it hides, because it reads
    as the name of a thing rather than as a shortened word: "app store", "in-app purchase", "admin panel",
    "spec review", "config file". Restore the letters.
    NOT A DEFECT: a capital that stands for no word at all -- "X-ray", "type 2", a model number. There is
    nothing to expand.

2.3 TERMS MUST BE NATIVE TO THE OCCUPATION. A term borrowed from an adjacent field signals the statement
    was drafted from the wrong template. Replace it with the occupation's own term.
    TEST: take each noun in turn and ask whose vocabulary it belongs to. A noun naming another trade's
    subject matter -- its livestock, its castings, its permits, its tolerances -- is borrowed even when
    the surrounding sentence is impeccable, and even when this occupation sometimes encounters the thing.
    Name what THIS occupation tracks about it instead.
2.4 STATEMENTS MUST BE COMPLETE. No mid-word or mid-clause truncation. Every statement is a grammatical
    sentence ending in a period.
2.5 A TERM MET ONLY IN ONE SPECIALTY DOES NOT BELONG IN AN OCCUPATION-LEVEL STATEMENT, whatever slot it
    fills — object, artifact, counterpart, procedure, statute, system, or setting. The statement describes
    the occupation; a term only one practice area's practitioners would use of their own work narrows the
    statement to that subfield and silently excludes everyone else.
    TEST: ask which practitioners could use this term of their own work. If only those in one practice
    area, sector, or client base could, replace it with the covering term at the occupation's level.
    AN ENUMERATED LIST OF SPECIALTY TERMS IS THE SAME DEFECT IN PLURAL, and the commoner shape, because it
    looks like coverage: each item is one practice area's instance, and the list silently excludes every
    specialty not on it.
    THE FIX KEEPS THE SHARED CORE BY NAME AND COVERS THE REST WITH ONE OPEN TAIL. An item every
    practitioner recognises stays; the specialty items fold into one covering phrase ("and other property
    owners", "or other regulated filings"). Where no item on the list is shared-core, the covering term
    replaces the whole list. The covering term must still be concrete — a role, an artifact kind, a
    category a reader can point to — never a collective that names nobody (2.1 and 5.2 still apply).
    NOT THIS: a concrete example inside a shared-core list is not a specialty term. "Such as" examples
    every practitioner recognises make a statement better (3.1a); the test is whether the term is met only
    inside one subfield, not whether it is specific.

## 3. SCOPE — ONE TASK, ONE ACTIVITY

3.1 OBJECT LISTS MUST BE CO-HYPONYMS. Every item in an "A, B, or C" list must name a thing the SAME
    activity is applied to. If one item is produced by a genuinely different process, it belongs in its
    own statement.
    TEST: substitute each item into the head on its own and ask whether the worker is doing the same thing
    each time. Same activity on different instances is ONE list, however varied the items look and however
    differently each reaches its recipient. A different activity splits out.
    Do NOT additionally require the items to match on workflow and on counterpart: that three-way test
    fails almost every real list and is the main source of wrong splits.
3.1a A LIST OF NEAR-SYNONYMS IS ONE ITEM. Co-hyponymy is necessary, not sufficient. Where two or three items
    name the same thing at slightly different scope or duration, they cost length and carry no information,
    and the sentence starts to read like a form rather than like the work. Collapse them to the plainest one
    and spend the words on a concrete example instead: one example a practitioner would recognise says more
    than three abstract near-equivalents.
    TEST: read the list in order and ask what item 2 tells a reader that item 1 did not. If the only answer is
    that it can be bigger, longer-term, more formal or more detailed, it is the same item twice — keep one.
    NOT THIS: this does not license dropping a genuinely different object to shorten a list. The items being
    collapsed have to be the same thing described twice, not two things the same activity is applied to.
3.1b OBJECT LISTS JOIN WITH "OR", NOT "AND". THE STATEMENT DESCRIBES AN OCCUPATION, NOT A PERSON, and that
    settles the conjunction. The list was assembled from activities reported by SEVERAL different workers, so
    it names what different practitioners do — one member handles the first kind of work on that list, another
    the second, a third neither. "A, B, and C" asserts that whoever does this task does all three, every time it occurs, which is
    false of an occupation by construction. "A, B, or C" says the activity applies to any of them, which is
    what a task inventory means.
    IT ALSO STOPS THE LIST READING AS SEPARATE DUTIES. A conjunction is how two duties are written, so "and"
    invites the reader to count the items; "or" tells them these are alternatives within one duty.
    TEST: put "any of" in front of the list. If the sentence is still a true description of the occupation's
    work, the list is disjunctive and takes "or". At occupation level almost every object list is — including
    ones a single worker really does handle together on a single occasion, because the next worker does not.
    THIS IS ABOUT OBJECTS, NOT VERBS. Verbs joined by "and" are governed by 3.2, which permits them where they
    are successive steps of one process — "draft and file", "record and submit". Do not change those.
    NOT THIS: the only objects that keep "and" are ones that are not alternatives at all — inseparable parts of
    a single artifact or act, where naming one without the others describes nothing anyone does.

3.2 VERB LISTS MUST NOT CONJOIN DISTINCT ACTIVITIES, AND SUCCESSIVE STEPS OF ONE PROCESS ARE NOT DISTINCT.
    Two shapes are acceptable. "Verb1 ... or Verb2 ..." where the verbs are ALTERNATIVE realisations of one
    activity, either of which finishes the job. And "Verb1 and Verb2 ..." where they are SUCCESSIVE STEPS of
    one process over the SAME object, ON ONE OCCASION — drafting a document and then checking it before it goes
    out, deciding an outcome and then telling whoever needs to know, fitting a part and then testing that it
    works. A worker who did the first and not the second has left one job half done, and a statement naming both
    describes that job completely rather than fusing two. Otherwise split the statement, or raise it to a
    genuine superordinate verb.
    NOT SUCCESSIVE, THOUGH IT SOUNDS IT: setting a rule and later applying it, making a plan and later working
    to it, producing a thing and later maintaining it. Each pair is one act done once and another done
    repeatedly afterwards, on occasions of its own — the second is not the finish of the first, it is the job
    the first made possible. Those split.
    BUT THE CHORES OF ONE ONGOING RESPONSIBILITY STAY TOGETHER, and this bounds the paragraph above: where
    the acts are the upkeep of one thing the worker is answerable for — recording entries and chasing the
    unresolved ones, directing daily work and periodically appraising it — no one of them is a duty on its
    own, and naming two or three of them describes the responsibility completely rather than fusing tasks.
    SUPERVISING ONE'S OWN STAFF IS THE COMMONEST SUCH RESPONSIBILITY, and its chores span both directions
    and all consequences: assigning and directing their work, answering their questions, unblocking their
    tasks, appraising their performance and settling what follows from the appraisal. A statement naming
    several of these is one supervisory duty described completely — question-and-answer inside the
    worker's own operation is not an exchange of sides, and an appraisal's consequences are not a second
    act.
    The produce-then-maintain split is for deliverables that leave the worker's own bookkeeping and are
    used by others.
    TEST: ask whether the second verb would ever be performed by someone who had NOT done the first, on a
    different occasion and for its own reason. If yes, they are separate duties and they split. If the second
    exists only because the first was done, they are one duty and they stay.
    NOT THIS: doing the work and WRITING ITS RESULT INTO A RECORD are not steps of one process. The recording
    is nearly always already a task of its own, so a statement naming both annexes that task's act — which is
    how one line comes to read as a duplicate of another.
3.3 REACTIVE AND GENERATIVE WORK ARE SEPARATE TASKS. Handling reported problems is a different activity
    from eliciting new requirements, even when both occur in the same conversation with the same
    counterpart.
3.4 OBJECT LISTS MUST BE DRAWN FROM THE OCCUPATION'S SHARED CORE, NOT ONE SPECIALTY. Where an occupation
    spans practice areas, specialty-specific objects narrow the statement to a subfield; generalise the
    list to the category that covers all of them.

## 4. PURPOSE, OCCASION AND METHOD CLAUSES

4.1 A PURPOSE CLAUSE MUST BE INFORMATIVE. Drop it where the verb already entails it.
    THE EASIEST CASE TO MISS IS THE MORPHOLOGICAL ONE: the clause is built on a word already standing in the
    head, so it restates it without repeating it. For a building surveyor, "measure floor areas to take
    measurements" and "schedule inspection visits to arrange scheduling" both say one thing twice, and neither
    reads as a restatement until you line the words up. CHECK THE STEMS, not the sense: if a noun or verb in
    your clause shares a root with a word in the head, the clause is the head with "to" in front. Say what the
    work FEEDS or DECIDES, or drop it.
    TEST: delete the clause and read the sentence again. If nothing was lost -- if a reader could have
    predicted the clause from the verb and object alone -- it was empty and it goes. Clauses naming what
    happens to anything sent anywhere ("for review", "for approval", "for their records") almost always
    fail this: submissions are reviewed by definition. Keep the clause only where it names the decision or
    outcome at stake, which a reader could not have guessed.
4.2 A PURPOSE CLAUSE MUST BE TRUE OF EVERY ITEM IN THE OBJECT LIST. If it holds for only part of the
    list, either drop the clause or split the statement.
4.2a AN OCCASION CLAUSE MUST NARROW WHEN THE WORK HAPPENS. "as needed", "when required", "in the course of
    the work", "on an ongoing basis", "during or after the working day" — these name no occasion at all. They
    are true of every moment the task could occur, so they add length and tell a reader nothing about when to
    expect the work.
    TEST: name an occasion the clause EXCLUDES. "before each shift" excludes mid-shift; "after an inspection
    finding" excludes the routine case; "at intake" excludes everything later. If you cannot name a time the
    clause rules out, delete it — the sentence is stronger without it.
    KEEP THE CLAUSE where the occasion is what makes this task different from a neighbouring one: the same act
    performed at a different trigger can be a different duty, and then the trigger is load-bearing and belongs
    in the sentence.

4.3 A METHOD CLAUSE MUST INFORM, AND AN INFORMATIVE ONE IS WELCOME. A "by ..." or "through ..." tail
    naming HOW the act is performed — the one or two moves that carry its judgement or skill ("by
    reviewing records and examining the patient") — is the standard O*NET shape for method
    and is LEGITIMATE even where the head alone already names the work: the method is the texture a reviewer
    wants. The clause is a DEFECT in exactly two cases: it REPEATS the head's stems ("measure areas by
    taking measurements" — check the roots, as 4.1 does), or it SMUGGLES A SECOND DUTY — a means item
    that is not a constituent move of the MAIN act but its own errand with its own occasion.
    IF THE HEAD GOES EMPTY without the clause — the "by ..." was carrying the only real content — the
    head was a category and the clause's contents are the actual tasks: promote one of them to the verb.
    NOT THIS, AND THE EXCEPTION IS NARROWER THAN IT SOUNDS. Naming a shared end and putting the activities
    after "through" is the repair for ONE defect: a statement whose activities need DIFFERENT VERBS and would
    read as two or three separate tasks if written as coordinate verb phrases. It does NOT apply to one verb
    over several objects — "prepare A, B, and C for X" is a plain object list and stays one.
    TEST THE EXCEPTION BEFORE CLAIMING IT: ask whether a single verb covers every activity. If one does, there
    was no defect to repair, and the "through" clause has traded a clean list for a category — which is the
    defect this principle exists to catch, arrived at from the other side. Never use the shape for a manner or
    a step.
    AND THE EXCEPTION IS NEVER A GERUND CHAIN. "by A-ing, B-ing, and C-ing" strings three activities behind
    the head the way a conjunction strings duties in front of it — the shape hides an enumeration, it does
    not repair one. Where the exception genuinely applies, name at most TWO activities after "through", or
    use the participial responsibility form of 1.4a; three or more "-ing" clauses after "by" or "through"
    is the defect itself, whatever the head says.

4.4 A STATE IS A GOAL, NOT A TASK. Some statements put a lasting condition where the object belongs — a
    disposition of people toward the worker or each other, a climate, a standing — something no single act
    produces, that persists between acts, and that no one could be watched doing. Work acts on an object a
    reader can see acted on; a condition like that is what the work is FOR. A statement whose object is such
    a condition has promoted its goal into the object slot and dropped the work: for a hotel concierge,
    "Build rapport with returning guests" names nothing a reader could watch. Write the observable act as
    the verb and object, and the condition as the purpose clause: "Greet returning guests by name and check
    in during their stay to build rapport." The activities name the acts; take the one or two that carry
    the duty.
    TEST: ask what the worker was seen DOING. If the sentence names no observable act, the work is missing
    and a goal is standing in its place.

## 5. COUNTERPARTS

5.1 INHERENTLY COORDINATIVE TASKS NAME THE COUNTERPART. Use published occupation titles where the
    counterpart is a specific occupation, and add "and other staff" to keep the statement open.
5.2 A GENERIC COLLECTIVE IS NOT A COUNTERPART. "stakeholders", "other parties", "personnel", "relevant teams",
    "decision-makers", "the business" — these name nobody. They tell a reader that somebody was involved without
    saying who, and they are to a counterpart what a bare category is to an object list: the reach without the
    evidence. Name the role, the function or the occupation; where the activities genuinely span several, name
    the two or three that recur and add "and other staff" to keep it open.
    A GENERIC WORD IS FINE AS THE OPEN TAIL, NEVER AS THE WHOLE COUNTERPART. 5.1 asks for that tail on purpose,
    so "the site foreman and other trades" is right while "other trades" alone is not — exactly as a category may
    head an object list but not replace it. Before flagging, check whether at least ONE named party appears
    beside the generic one. If one does, the sentence has its counterpart and the tail is doing its job.
    TEST: could a reader go and find the person? For a building firm, "the site foreman" and "the parts
    supplier" are findable; "stakeholders" is not. If the word survives being swapped for any other group in
    any other trade, it is naming nobody.
    THIS ONE IS WORTH ITS OWN LINE because a reviewer rejects it on sight: it reads as a statement written
    without asking who the work was for, and one such word can sink an otherwise sound sentence.
5.3 A COUNTERPART MUST BE SHARED-CORE, NOT SPECIALTY. This is 2.5 applied to the counterpart slot: a
    counterpart met only in one specialty's cases narrows the statement to that subfield the same way a
    specialty object does (3.4). Keep the counterpart every practitioner meets by name and fold specialty
    counterparts into the open tail (5.1); where none is shared-core, the covering role replaces the list.
    For a veterinarian, "negotiate treatment plans with dairy herd owners, racehorse trainers, or kennel
    operators" is three specialties, not the occupation — "with animal owners" covers them and everyone
    unlisted. The tail must still ride on a findable named party, never a collective (5.2).
