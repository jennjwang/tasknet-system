Two task statements from ONE occupation's published task list were flagged as possibly overlapping. Decide
ONE thing: do these two lines' CLAIMS overlap — is there an act that appears on both? You are ROUTING, not
merging. Whether an overlapping pair is one duty the inventory owes once, or two duties whose boundary needs
rewording, is decided further down the chain BY THE MEMBER ACTIVITIES under each label, not by you: you see
two sentences and nothing else, which is the right instrument for the routing question and the wrong one for
the merge question. Answer the routing question and stop.

THE TEST IS HOW THE TWO LINES READ. These statements ARE the deliverable — almost every reader will see them and
never see the activities beneath them. So ask: reading these two lines side by side, would a worker in this
occupation say "that is the same job duty, written twice"? If yes, say YES. Do not ask how long the list is or
whether it "has room" for both; a duplicate reads as a duplicate in a list of ten or a hundred.
Ask it about the WORK, not the sentences. Two statements can share almost no words and still be one duty,
because each writer reached for different instances, different paperwork, or a different moment in the cycle.

DECIDE IN THIS ORDER. STEPS 1-2 ARE A GATE, NOT FACTORS TO WEIGH.

STEP 1 — LIST THE ACTS EACH LINE NAMES. A line can name more than one: read its lead verb AND any act in an
"and ..." clause or a trailing clause. Collapse near-synonyms into one act before comparing (record, log, enter,
file, maintain and document are ONE act; plan, prepare and develop are ONE act; assess, evaluate and grade are
ONE act).

STEP 2 — IF NO ACT APPEARS ON BOTH LISTS, ANSWER NO AND STOP. This is the case this judge gets wrong most
often: measured against hand labels it answered YES to seven pairs in a row whose acts were disjoint. A shared
OBJECT, a shared POPULATION served, a shared DATA SOURCE, a shared SETTING or a shared PURPOSE is NOT a shared
act. Delivering a service and inspecting one are two acts. Working out what is wrong and instructing someone are
two acts. Informing a client and building rapport with them are two acts. Producing a figure and advising
someone what to do about it are two acts. In every one of those the two lines share their object and still
describe different work, and a published inventory owes both lines.

WHEN YOU GENUINELY CANNOT TELL, ANSWER YES. You are the FIRST of two judges. A wrong YES costs one look at
the pair's member activities downstream, where the two lines are read against the concrete work beneath them
and the default is to keep both — a wrong YES publishes nothing. A wrong NO is FINAL: the pair is never looked
at again, and if the claims do overlap the inventory ships the overlap. The two errors are not symmetric, so
uncertainty resolves to YES.
THIS IS NOT A LICENCE TO SKIP STEP 2, AND IT DOES NOT SOFTEN IT. The tie-break applies ONLY where you cannot
tell whether an act appears on both lists. Where you CAN tell and it does not — where the only thing shared is
an object, a population served, a data source, a setting, a tool, a document format, or a carrier verb — the
answer is NO and it is not close. Answering YES on act-disjoint pairs is the failure this judge is measured on:
against hand labels it once answered YES to seven such pairs in a row. Doubt about a SHARED ACT resolves to
yes; the absence of one does not.

STEP 3 — IF AN ACT APPEARS ON BOTH LISTS, THE ANSWER IS YES. If ONE side names an act the other also names,
that is enough, even when each side names a second act of its own — the overlap is what matters, not the
surplus. The shapes below are what a shared act LOOKS LIKE; they are not a further test to pass. They fall in
two families and BOTH ANSWER YES, because both are pairs a reader could file the same activity under.

SAY YES — one duty written twice:
* THE SAME ACT ON DIFFERENT SPECIALTIES, PRACTICE AREAS, CASE TYPES, OR CLIENT SEGMENTS. This list describes an
  OCCUPATION, not a person, so the subject the work is applied to is which INSTANCE a given member happens to
  see — not a second duty. Two lines that name one act and differ only in the field it is aimed at are one line
  the inventory owes once, with the fields as examples. It does not matter that the two specialisms need
  different bodies of knowledge, or that no single member does both: an occupation-level inventory that carries
  one line per specialty hides the activity they share, and the most widespread work in the job then appears
  nowhere as itself.
* SAME ACTION, SAME OBJECT, different WORDS FOR THE SAME SET. For a groundskeeper, "Trim hedges, shrubs, and
  borders" and "Trim ornamental plantings, such as topiary or screening hedges" are one duty written twice —
  the two lists name the SAME plantings, not two groups of them.
  BUT A NARROWING MODIFIER IS NOT A DIFFERENT EXAMPLE. If one side's object is the other's object PLUS a
  qualifier that CUTS DOWN THE SET OF CASES — a scope, a horizon, a named subgroup — the narrower side
  is a SUB-TYPE, and sub-types are TWO duties. A STAGE OF THE SURROUNDING PROCESS IS NOT SUCH A QUALIFIER:
  it says WHEN along a sequence the same act happens, and the act is the same act at every point, so it
  does not cut the cases down. A different TRIGGER does cut them down — see the dimensions above.
  A MODIFIER THAT NAMES WHERE THE ACT LANDS IS NOT A NARROWING. Saying which system, file, register, tool or
  format the same act is performed into or with does not cut the set of cases down — it says the same thing more
  concretely. "Record the scores" and "enter the scores into the district system" are ONE act on ONE object; the
  system is where it goes, not a subgroup of it. Same for a named tool, a named form, or a named format.
  THE TEST, and apply it to the CASES rather than the words: would a worker doing the broader side ever be
  doing something the narrower side does NOT cover? If the only thing the narrower side adds is where the work
  lands or what it is done with, they are one duty. If it covers only SOME of the triggers or subgroups the
  other covers, they are two — but covering only some STAGES is not a difference.
* One names the ARTIFACT produced, the other the ACTIVITY that produces it, over the same work.

ALSO SAY YES for these — two duties, but you only reach them when an act appears on both lists, so the
lines' claims overlap and a reader could file the same activity under either. The chain will hold them apart
on the members; your job is only to route them there, so do NOT withhold a YES because the work divides:
* BUILDING OR SETTING SOMETHING UP, against MAINTAINING OR FIXING IT AFTERWARDS. These are two acts separated
  in time. Creating a plan, a document or a system is not the
  same act as revising it once it is in use, even when the same worker owns both and even when the second act
  reads as a continuation of the first. Two lines, not one.
* SUB-TYPES OF ONE OBJECT ARE TWO DUTIES, and so is ONE ACT FOR TWO READERS OR FROM TWO TRIGGERS. A worker handling two categories of the same document, or
  performing one act for two different audiences, or performing it once because something went wrong and again
  because it was scheduled, is doing work a published inventory owes TWO lines. Do not merge on a shared act
  alone — the reader and the trigger are distinguishing, not incidental.
  A TRIGGER IS NOT A STAGE, AND ONLY THE TRIGGER DIVIDES. A trigger is the event that makes the work start; it
  divides because it can change what the worker does. A STAGE is a position in a sequence that ONE act runs
  through — the early instance of a thing and the late one — and it does NOT divide, because the act is the
  same act at both. If the only difference between two candidates is how far along the surrounding process
  they sit, that is ONE duty with both moments as instances.
  A SECOND EXCEPTION, AND IT IS THE SPECIALTY CASE ABOVE: a sub-type that is only the PRACTICE AREA, CASE TYPE
  or CLIENT SEGMENT the act is aimed at does not divide the duty. Two categories of one document are two duties
  when handling each is a different job — a different trigger, a different reader, a different act. They are
  ONE duty when the only difference is which field of the occupation's work the document belongs to, because
  that is which instance a member happens to see. Ask what the worker does differently, not what the document
  is called: if the answer is "the same steps, on a different subject", it is one line.
* GENUINELY DIFFERENT ACTIONS, even on the same object — for a mechanic, inspecting brakes and replacing brake
  pads are separate duties: different tools, different skills, either can happen without the other. This holds
  when one act ordinarily follows the other in the same cycle: diagnosing a fault and repairing it are two
  duties, and a shared object noun does not make them one.
* Work only some members do, where the ACT ITSELF is one those members alone perform — a distinct procedure or
  authority, not the same procedure aimed at a different subject.
A DIFFERENT NOUN IS NOT ENOUGH. Before refusing the merge, name what a worker would actually have to do
differently. If the only thing you can name is that the two statements mention different items or different
paperwork, the answer is YES. A different ACT is enough to refuse the merge, even inside one cycle — the
question that remains is whether an act still appears on both lists.

ANSWER WITH ONE WORD:
  yes  an act appears on BOTH lists — either shape family above. One duty written twice, or two duties whose
       claims overlap: both answer yes, because both need the pair looked at against its members.
  no   distinct duties with no shared act: you stopped at step 2, or the only thing shared is a field,
       a setting, a tool, a document format, or a carrier verb (manage, handle, conduct, support)
       standing in for different work.

Answer with ONLY 'yes' or 'no'.
