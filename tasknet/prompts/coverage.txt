You are judging whether one specific worker task (the ATOM) falls under a broader O*NET-style task statement
(the LABEL), for tasks within a single occupation.

The ATOM FALLS UNDER the LABEL when performing the atom is ONE WAY of doing the label — an instance, a sub-type,
or a constituent step of the SAME activity, and no broader than the label.

THE DEFAULT ANSWER IS YES. Below is the COMPLETE list of things that make it NO. Work through the two reading
steps, then check the four disqualifiers. If none of the four applies, answer YES. Do not invent a fifth.

READING STEP A — SPLIT EACH SIDE INTO ACT, OBJECT, AND PURPOSE.
  ACT = the verb, what the worker does. OBJECT = what it is done to. PURPOSE = everything after "to …",
  "for …", "so that …" — the reason it is performed. Do this for the label and for the atom.

READING STEP B — NOW DISCARD BOTH PURPOSE CLAUSES.
  Labels are written to stand alone and almost always state a purpose. Atoms are terse notes from a worker and
  usually leave it implicit. The atom's silence about purpose is therefore evidence of NOTHING, and a purpose it
  does state in unlike words is evidence of nothing either.
    · "Review medical and accident records for PIP arbitrations" and "Review investigative and medical records
      to assess case facts and damages" are the SAME ACT ON THE SAME OBJECT; one states a purpose and one does
      not, and that difference is invisible here.
  Never answer NO because the atom omits the label's purpose, lacks its purpose verb ("assess", "evaluate",
  "determine", "improve", "ensure"), or gives a different-sounding reason for the same act. Judge ACT and OBJECT.

THE FOUR DISQUALIFIERS. Check each against the ACT and OBJECT you extracted.

D1 — A DIFFERENT ACT. The atom's act is not the label's act and is not a constituent step of it. Sharing a
   tool, a topic, a field or a client is not sharing an act.
   NOT a different act: a label joining several acts with "and", "or" or a comma list is satisfied by ANY ONE
   of them — never require every conjunct, and never require every listed object. A "such as …" /
   "including …" list is ILLUSTRATIVE of one CATEGORY, not a closed checklist: an atom of the same kind
   qualifies even when unlisted ("operate shop machinery, such as saws" covers a lathe). A
   constituent step qualifies: a physical exam is part of "evaluate patients".

D2 — THE ATOM IS BROADER. The atom's act or scope exceeds the label's, so the label describes only part of it.
   Specificity runs ONE WAY only: an atom naming a NARROWER counterpart, object, instrument, setting,
   specialty or client type still falls under the label. The label is not required to anticipate the instance.
   Only the reverse — a broader atom — disqualifies.

D3 — THE OTHER SIDE OF AN EXCHANGE. The label puts the worker on one side (performing, requesting, sending,
   authoring) and the atom puts them on the other (checking someone else's performance, granting, receiving,
   approving). However much vocabulary they share, these are different work.

D4 — A DIFFERENT PRODUCT, AND THIS ONE IS CONDITIONAL. It applies ONLY where the label's act produces a THING:
   a document, a delivered service, a configured system, a released build. Then an atom producing an artifact
   of a different kind, or work the label never reaches, is disqualified; an atom producing an input to or an
   earlier stage of that thing is NOT.
   D4 DOES NOT APPLY WHERE THE LABEL'S PRODUCT IS A JUDGEMENT. If the label's act is analysing, assessing,
   reviewing, evaluating or advising, its product IS a conclusion, an opinion, a recommendation or a decision.
   An atom that reaches a conclusion, decides what to do next, or recommends a course is then producing
   EXACTLY what the label produces. Do not disqualify it.

VERDICT. If D1, D2, D3 and D4 all fail to apply, the answer is YES — including when you are unsure, because
work described in two registers, a worker's terse note and a written task statement, is more often the same
work than different work.
