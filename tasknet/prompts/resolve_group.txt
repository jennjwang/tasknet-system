THAT TAXONOMY DECIDES WHICH DIFFERENCES MAY HOLD TWO TASKS APART HERE, and it is the same one the pair judges
upstream are held to, so a partition grounded outside it puts this call at odds with the evidence that reached
it. A difference listed under THESE DO NOT DIVIDE is not a reason to refuse a MERGE, however unlike the two
labels look — subject matter, job type, customer segment, the kind of artifact where its function is the same,
and the counterpart's identity are all differences that belong INSIDE one entry as its examples.

JUDGE THE ACTIVITIES, NOT THE SENTENCES. Two statements can share most of their vocabulary and still be separate
duties. Check, concretely, for every pair inside a candidate MERGE group:
  - WHAT ACT is performed — the steps, the judgement, the skill it takes.
  - WHETHER either side's activities would still be owed if the other were done. If yes, they are separate —
    do not fold them into the same group merely because a THIRD task in the component also collides with both.

WHEN YOU SEPARATE, lead the new statement with the ONE thing that divides it from its neighbours — the act, the
object, the stage, the counterpart, or the output — taken from its own members, never from an industry,
employer, sector, or product qualifier. THE TEST: read the rewritten label against a NEIGHBOUR's example
members. If it would still be a fair description of that neighbour's work, it has not separated anything.

WHEN YOU MERGE, the single statement must cover what every member of the group describes. Do not climb to a
category wide enough to hold them by saying nothing, and do not simply concatenate the labels with "and".

LABEL SIMILARITY IS NOT MERGE EVIDENCE, AND WITHOUT A CONFIRMED PAIR THE DEFAULT IS SEPARATE. Every pair below
reached you because two LABELS read alike — that is why they were flagged, so it cannot also be the reason to

this the same work described twice", and a small task sitting entirely inside a larger one is not the same

task is an instance of work the other task's statement and members already describe — the same act, merely
fewer instances — and the other task has more besides, MERGE them as

WHAT THE PRODUCT DOES IS NOT A DIVIDER — the taxonomy does not carry it as one, and neither does this
call: any two artifacts can be told apart by their function after the fact, which made "product
function" a rubber stamp on every separation. Where the members show the same act — the same steps, the
same judgement, the same skill — the tasks are one duty however differently their products function, and
the products belong inside the merged statement as its examples. Only a difference in what the WORKER DOES
holds two colliding tasks apart.
AND THE ACT IS THE MOVES, NOT THE CONTEXT. Bargaining is one act: negotiating the terms of a deal and
negotiating the resolution of a dispute exchange positions, trade concessions, and converge on agreed
terms by the same steps, the same judgement, the same skill — the deal or the dispute is the subject the
act is applied to, and a subject never divides. The same reading applies to any verb tempted to split by
its object's situation: advising on a plan and advising on a problem, inspecting on intake and inspecting
on complaint are one act each unless the STEPS themselves change. Name an act difference only where you
can say what the worker DOES differently, move by move. A pair for which you cannot
name a divider is one duty by the taxonomy's own test, and the answer for it is MERGE, not a cautious
SEPARATE: caution that cannot say what it is protecting is not caution, it is an overlap shipped to the
published list. Label similarity still counts for nothing in either direction — the divider must hold
between the MEMBERS, not the sentences.

DO NOT LOOK FOR THE SAME ACTIVITY ON BOTH SIDES. Every activity is filed under exactly ONE task, so the member
lists below are disjoint BY CONSTRUCTION and no activity can appear twice. The question is never "does this
activity appear on both sides", it is whether the two SETS are one duty split or two duties.

THE COUNTERPART'S IDENTITY DOES NOT DIVIDE, AND IT DOES NOT UNITE EITHER. Advising a client, a supervising
attorney and a regulator is one act with three recipients — a different name on the recipient is not a second
duty. But neither is a shared recipient a reason to merge: two things done FOR the same people can still be two
acts. What divides is what the worker DOES and what the product DOES.

BUILDING SOMETHING AND REVISING IT LATER ARE TWO ACTS. Creating a plan, document or system is not the same act
as adjusting it once in use — SEPARATE, even where one worker owns both. THIS IS THE TAXONOMY'S
DELIVERABLE-AND-ITS-AFTERLIFE CASE AND IT STOPS AT THE CHORES BULLET: acts that are the upkeep of one ongoing
responsibility — the entries and the review of one set of books, the directing and the appraising of one
supervisory charge — are ONE duty however far apart their occasions fall, and this rule is not a reason to
hold them apart.

TEACHING AND AUTHORISING ARE TWO ACTS. Explaining, coaching or mentoring over time is not the same act as
approving, signing off or overruling a decision in the moment, even with the same counterpart and the same
subject. If one side's activities are ongoing formation and the other's are point-in-time decisions, SEPARATE.

A DIFFERENT LIST OF SPECIFICS IS ENOUGH ONLY WHERE THE SPECIFICS THEMSELVES DO DIFFERENT THINGS. Where both
sides' named artifacts are instances of ONE kind doing ONE job, the two lists are not a difference — and A
DIFFERENT BODY OF RULES, SPECIALTY, OR CLIENT SITUATION IS NEVER THE DIFFERENCE: that is the subject
matter the same act is applied to, and specialists in different subjects perform the same task on them.
ARTIFACTS THAT DO ONE JOB ARE ONE KIND: where every artifact both sides name exists to do the same job —
each one records, establishes, or directs an arrangement its parties are bound by — composing them is ONE
duty however far apart their subjects sit and however different their names.

THE HETEROGENEITY RULE: FIND THE LEVEL THE OCCUPATION SHARES. An occupation spans practices, and a
colliding pair is often ONE act refracted through that heterogeneity — the same work appearing as one
practice's version and another practice's version. The published level is the one the whole occupation
can stand behind:
  · Where the collision tracks the occupation's DIVISION OF LABOR — different practices each doing their
    variant of the act — the right entry is ONE task at the covering abstraction, with each practice's
    subjects as its examples. Splitting there publishes specialties, not tasks.
PRECEDENCE WHEN THE RULES COLLIDE: THE WORKER EVIDENCE OUTRANKS A NAMEABLE ACT DIVIDER. A divider can be
articulated for almost any pair — that is why SEPARATE carries a burden at all — so a difference in how
the act can be described does not defeat the division-of-labor evidence. Where the evidence line says
DIFFERENT people, the pair is one act refracted through practices and the answer is MERGE to the covering
abstraction, unless the divider is one of the hard structural ones: the SIDE OF AN EXCHANGE with an
opposing party, or a DELIVERABLE AND ITS AFTERLIFE. Weigh the evidence first; reach for a divider only when it
survives it.
A SEPARATE VERDICT ON A DIFFERENT-PEOPLE PAIR MUST SHOW ITS WORK IN THE DIVIDER FIELD: name the hard
structural divider (the side of an exchange with an opposing party, a deliverable and its afterlife) that
lets it survive the precedence rule, or say concretely what the worker DOES differently move by move. A
DIFFERENT-ACT divider that is really the same moves applied to a different SUBJECT — a deal against a
dispute, one practice's documents against another's — does not survive, and neither does one that is
really a different KIND OF ARTIFACT — a formal written instrument against an outline for live use, one
filing form against another — where composing them takes the same judgement: either way the pair is one
act refracted through the occupation's division of labor, and the verdict for it is MERGE to the
covering abstraction.
THE CORPUS WORKER SETS BELOW MEASURE THAT DIVISION OF LABOR. Disjoint workers: practice-split
variants of one act — merge up. This is not literally about who
contributed which atom; it is the occupation's structure showing through the sample. One shared worker of
many is coincidence; weigh the proportion of the SMALLER task's workers.
WHERE THE EVIDENCE SAYS SAME PEOPLE, the shared workers are testimony that the two bodies of work
COEXIST in one job — each held at its own scale — which supports SEPARATE for peers and warns against a
containment fold; the same people doing both is how an occupation carries two duties, not proof they are
one.

REPEATED REWORDING FAILURE IS MERGE EVIDENCE — WEIGHED, NOT DECISIVE. Where a pair's evidence line
records STRAIN — reworded to separate and still reading as one duty — the collision is unlikely to be a
wording accident: each failed rewrite strengthens the evidence that the occupation's vocabulary has SO
FAR refused to state the difference in one sentence each, and a published level made of single sentences
cannot carry a distinction its labels cannot state. Weigh strain beside the worker evidence as support
for ONE entry, in proportion to the count. Two cautions bound it: a CROSS-CLAIM line means one label
borrows vocabulary that is core to the OTHER side's members — that is a REPAIR case, not merge evidence,
and the borrowing label should be fixed before the collision is read as fusion; and a pair whose labels
were materially rewritten since the last failure has earned a fresh test, which the count already
reflects. Only a hard structural divider (the side of an exchange with an opposing party, a deliverable
and its afterlife) overrides strain outright.
