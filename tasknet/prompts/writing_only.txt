A trained occupational analyst is reviewing a published task statement. Predict whether the analyst will

WHOSE WORK THIS IS IS NOT YOUR QUESTION. Whether a neighbouring occupation does it, whether it is a
supervisor's job, how many workers do it — none of that can make a well-built sentence faulty or a
faulty sentence sound. A statement describing somebody else's work entirely is, for your purposes,
CLEAN if the sentence is sound. You are judging the words.

The occupation's published task list is given below FOR ONE PURPOSE ONLY: a sentence can claim an

## NAME THE RULE YOU ARE APPLYING

Every objection belongs to exactly one numbered section above, and `principle` is that section's name:

  verb         §1 VERB SELECTION — the verb claims an authority the occupation does not hold or
               understates one it does, names the forum instead of the work, or sits above the
               activities' own verb when a sharper one is available. ALSO §1.4-1.4d: a container verb
               from the offender list ("manage", "handle", "conduct", "oversee", "deal with", "pursue", "ensure",
               "perform", "execute", "use", "utilize");
               TWO OR MORE "-ing" clauses stacked in one statement (§1.4b — count them: a second
               participial clause is the defect, whatever introduces it; ONE "by X-ing" MEANS clause
               naming how the main verb's act is performed is allowed and NOT a defect, including up to
               TWO coordinated gerunds inside it; THREE OR MORE gerunds in the clause is the
               enumeration defect and IS flagged); a statement running well past
               ~20 words (§1.4c); or a mandate verb ("represent", "serve", "act for") leading where a
               concrete act should, outside §1.4d's exceptions.
               BE ESPECIALLY WARY OF THE CHORE-ENUMERATION SHAPE: a responsibility head trailed by a
               procedural inventory of its own chores — "including X-ing records, Y-ing responses, and
               Z-ing with counterparts", or the same list dressed as nouns. Walking the reader through
               the steps is the enumeration defect whatever the grammar; the members carry that detail,
               and §1.4b says the chores are DROPPED first. A statement that cannot say what the worker
               is answerable for without listing how they go about it draws this objection.
  vocabulary   §2 VOCABULARY — house jargon, a clipped or abbreviated form, a term borrowed from an
               adjacent field, a truncated statement.
  fusion       §3.2 / §3.3 — the statement conjoins ACTIVITIES that are separate duties, or fuses reactive
               with generative work. Two or more verbs, each with its own outcome.
  objects      §3.1 / §3.1a / §3.1b / §3.4 — the OBJECT LIST is malformed: items that are not co-hyponyms,
               two items that are near-synonyms of each other, "and" where alternatives want "or", or a
               list drawn from ONE SPECIALTY where the occupation spans several (§3.4 — generalise to the
               category that covers all of them).
               §3.4 HAS A TEST YOU CAN RUN ON THE ACTIVITY LIST YOU WERE GIVEN. Take each item in the
               statement's object list and find the activities that name it. If an item is named by ONE
               activity while the rest of the list is named by several, that item is one worker's specialty
               promoted into the statement, and the task has been narrowed to their subfield — say `objects`
               and quote it. The category that covers what all the activities name is what belongs there.
               A WELL-FORMED OBJECT LIST IS NOT A DEFECT AND IS NOT `fusion`. "unit, integration, and
               automated tests", "charts, notes, and clinical documentation", "toileting, incontinence care,
               and bathing" are one action over co-hyponyms, which §3.1 asks for. Naming several things the
               SAME act applies to is the statement being specific, and specificity is wanted. Flag the list
               only when you can say WHICH of the four rules above it breaks and quote the offending item.
  clauses      §4 PURPOSE, OCCASION AND METHOD CLAUSES — an uninformative purpose clause, a purpose that
               is untrue of part of the object list, an occasion clause that narrows nothing, or a
               "by ..." / "through ..." tail that repeats the head's stems or smuggles a second duty.
               A "by X-ing" clause enumerating the MEANS of the main act (§4.3) is legitimate and NOT
               a defect, even where the head alone names the work.
               ALSO §4.4, WHICH RUNS THE OTHER WAY: a lasting CONDITION standing in the OBJECT slot — a
               disposition of people toward the worker or each other, a climate, a standing; something
               that persists between acts and that no one could be watched doing — with no observable
               act anywhere in the sentence. The goal has been promoted into the object and the work
               dropped; the activity list you were given names the acts the statement should lead with.
               Say `clauses` and quote the abstraction.
  counterpart  §5 COUNTERPARTS — a coordinative task that does not name who it is done with, or names a
               generic collective ("stakeholders", "other parties", "relevant teams") instead of a
               counterpart.
  acronym      AN INITIALISM OR ACRONYM LEFT UNEXPANDED. Say `acronym` and quote the letters. A term whose
               expansion IS the jargon is still `acronym`: write the words and let §2 judge them.
               THE TEST IS STRUCTURAL. Take every run of capitals in the sentence and ask one question of
               each: do these letters stand for words? If they do it is an initialism and it is a defect --
               whatever the letters are, however short, and whether or not you recognise the thing they
               name. Ask it of each token you find rather than looking for terms you have seen before.
               BREVITY AND FAMILIARITY ARE NOT EXEMPTIONS. A two-letter form reads as an ordinary word
               rather than as initials; a form ubiquitous in the field stops looking like initials at all;
               a form naming a well-known method or statute looks like a name. Each is still letters
               standing for words. Slow down on the short ones.
               DOMAIN VOCABULARY THAT IS NOT AN INITIALISM IS NOT THIS. "Hoyer lift", "Medicaid",
               "gait belt" are words a practitioner says; they are not abbreviations and are not a defect.
  face_valid   WOULD A PRACTITIONER RECOGNISE THIS AS HOW THE WORK IS DESCRIBED? The statement is true,
               correctly scoped, and still reads as something no one in the occupation would say — an
               assembled O*NET-ese construction, a noun-heavy paraphrase of a plain act, or a framing
               borrowed from the list's own template rather than from the work. This is the one axis that
               is about RECOGNITION rather than a rule, so it carries a higher bar: only say `face_valid`
               when you can name what a practitioner would say instead. Do NOT use it as a home for
               awkwardness you cannot otherwise place — that is what the paragraph below forbids.

Pick the section the defect actually offends. Do NOT reach for a label because the sentence merely reads
awkwardly — awkwardness is not a section, and if no section is offended there is no defect. `face_valid`
is not an exception to that: it needs the sentence a practitioner would have written instead.

## FIRM OBJECTIONS VERSUS QUESTIONS

Objections come in two strengths.

Three are FIRM: a false claim about what the worker does or decides (verb), a clipped word (vocabulary),
two separate jobs in one sentence (fusion). The analyst states these flatly and asks for the fix. Predict
a defect on these whenever you can quote the words.

AND THE AUTHORITY CLAIM IS THE ONE MOST OFTEN MISSED, so test it explicitly on every statement rather
than waiting for it to look wrong. Find the sentence's main verb and any verb in its purpose clause, and
ask of each: does the published list above show this occupation MAKING that call, or only feeding it?
A verb that settles something — determine, approve, authorise, set, decide, evaluate-in-order-to-decide —
when the list shows the occupation advising, preparing, or recommending, is a false claim, and the softer
verb is wanted. The reverse also counts: a verb that merely advises where the list shows the occupation
doing the thing understates it, and the stronger verb is wanted. Both are defects of the VERB. Name the
verb to change; do not conclude anything about who owns the task.

House vocabulary (vocabulary) is a QUESTION rather than a firm objection: the analyst is asking whether a
term is in general use or can be simplified, not asserting that it is wrong. So:
  · A term that is ordinary English used in a slightly specialised way, or a widely current name for a
    thing, is NOT a defect. An analyst recognises those and passes them.
  · A term that only makes sense inside a particular workplace's process, or that reads as a metaphor
    or a piece of internal shorthand, IS one. It is the kind of word a reader would have to be told.
  When you are unsure which of those two a term is, say there is no defect.

## CALIBRATION

A defect is the exception, not the rule. It needs a trigger you can quote. Do not accumulate mild
reservations into an objection: name one identifiable thing, or nothing. But do not shrink from a clear one
either: a verb that claims a decision the sentence gives no basis for (verb), a truncated word
(vocabulary), two jobs joined by "and" (fusion), or a coordinative task whose counterpart is
"stakeholders" (counterpart) are all defects however fluent the sentence reads.
