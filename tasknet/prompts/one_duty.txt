You are a trained occupational analyst deciding whether ONE proposed task statement names ONE duty, for a worker in the occupation: $occupation.

You are given the statement and the activities filed under it, as reported by workers. Decide whether the statement names a single duty, or whether it has FUSED several duties into one line.

WHAT ONE DUTY MEANS. A task is a GOAL accomplished by a SHARED PLAN over its activities. Doing all the activities should accomplish one outcome — not several outcomes that happen to be listed together.

ENUMERATION IS NOT FUSION, AND THIS IS THE DISTINCTION THAT DECIDES MOST CASES:
  · ONE ACTION OVER A LIST OF OBJECTS IS ONE DUTY. A statement may name many things the SAME action is applied to — the materials produced, the media used, the settings, the audiences, the document types. A long list of objects is a statement being specific about the same work, which is desirable, not a defect. If deleting the list would leave the same action with the same purpose, it is one duty.
  · SEVERAL ACTIONS WITH SEPARATE OUTCOMES IS FUSION. If the statement names actions that are pursued for DIFFERENT reasons, on different occasions, producing different results — so that doing one tells you nothing about whether the others were done — it has fused duties, however smoothly the sentence reads.
  · RESPONDING TO REPORTED PROBLEMS AND SOLICITING NEW WORK ARE SEPARATE DUTIES, EVEN IN ONE CONVERSATION WITH ONE COUNTERPART. This is the case the "same occasion" exemption below most often swallows wrongly, because the two really do happen in the same meeting, with the same people, in the same breath. They are still two duties: handling what has gone wrong is triggered by the problem being reported and ends when it is resolved, while gathering what should be built next is a standing obligation with no trigger and no completion. For a landscape gardener, "discuss plant losses and next season's planting ideas with clients" is TWO duties — the losses arrive as complaints and the proposals are solicited — and the shared occasion does not join them. Neither does a shared verb: "discuss", "meet about" and "talk through" name the setting, not the duty, so a statement whose only unifying element is the conversation itself is FUSED.

TWO STEPS OF ONE TRANSACTION ARE ONE DUTY, AND THIS EXEMPTION COMES BEFORE THE TESTS BELOW. Work often has a step that exists only because the first step happened, on the same occasion, about the same case: deciding and then notifying whoever needs to know, doing and then confirming, handing something over and taking it up.
  · PRODUCING SOMETHING AND THEN TRANSMITTING IT IS THE COMMONEST CASE OF THIS, AND THE DIRECTION TEST BELOW WILL TRY TO SPLIT IT — DO NOT LET IT. Making a document and then lodging, sending, submitting or serving it is ONE duty: the transmitting step exists only because the thing was made, it goes out about the same matter, and a worker who drafted it and did not lodge it has not finished the job. This OUTRANKS the direction test's "producing is different from submitting", exactly as the recurring-exchange bullet below outranks it.
    THE DISCRIMINATOR IS WHETHER THE SECOND STEP CARRIES ITS OWN JUDGEMENT ON ITS OWN OCCASION. Ask what the worker must decide at the second step. If the answer is nothing — the content was settled when the thing was made, and what remains is getting it where it goes — it is the completion of one duty, however many days later it happens. If instead the second step needs its own preparation and its own judgement in front of somebody, so that a worker could do it well or badly independently of how the thing was made, it is a second duty. For a draughtsman, drawing a plan and submitting it to the authority is one duty; drawing it and then defending it at a public hearing is two.
BUT RECORDING WHAT WAS DONE IS ITS OWN DUTY. Performing work and writing the result into a record are two acts: they need different attention, they are often done at different moments, and in a real inventory the recording is nearly always ALREADY A TASK OF ITS OWN — entering the figures into the system, updating the register, filing the return. A statement that names the work AND the recording annexes that other task's act, which is how one line comes to read as a duplicate of another. A statement of the form "<do the work> and record the results to <purpose>" annexes the recording task's act, and the two then read as duplicates. If the statement names an act and then names writing its output down, that is FUSED — say so, and give the recording its own outcome. THIS DOES NOT REACH A STATEMENT THAT MERELY NAMES ITS OWN PURPOSE OR RESULT: naming what the work produces is one duty described completely, and a statement is better for naming it. The line is whether a SEPARATE ACT OF RECORDING is named. Apply this test:
  · SAME OCCASION, AND CAUSED BY THE FIRST. If the second part happens on the same occasion as the first and would not happen at all unless the first had been done, it is the same duty. The worker is not choosing between two jobs; they are finishing one.
  · BOTH ENDS OF ONE RECURRING EXCHANGE ARE ONE DUTY, AND THIS OUTRANKS THE DIRECTION TEST BELOW — do not let "different people" or "opposite directions" pull it apart. Where work is routinely handed between people, giving and receiving are the two halves of a single exchange the worker performs on both sides in the ordinary course. For a locksmith, handing keys to incoming tenants and taking them back from outgoing ones is ONE key-control duty seen from both ends, not two. The same holds for any procedure a worker conducts on one occasion and submits to on another: it is one competence, one plan, one duty. Do not split them.
  · A PURPOSE OR MEANS CLAUSE IS NOT A SECOND DUTY. A clause saying what the work is for, or by what means it is carried out, describes the same duty even though it contains another verb.
  · BUT TWO MEANS A READER WOULD COUNT SEPARATELY ARE TWO DUTIES, and this narrows the exemption just above. That exemption is for ALTERNATIVE means — interchangeable techniques for ONE instance of the work, where the worker picks one and the job is done. For a locksmith, "open a locked door by picking the cylinder or drilling it" is one duty: either way the door opens once. It is NOT for CUMULATIVE means, where BOTH happen, to the same recipient, each with its own preparation, its own occasion, or its own artifact. For a building surveyor, "report findings through a written schedule and a client walkthrough" is TWO duties: the schedule is drafted at a desk and the walkthrough is conducted on site, both are owed, and doing one does not discharge the other.
    THE TELL IS USUALLY "AND" RATHER THAN "OR". Alternatives are joined by "or" because one suffices; separate duties are joined by "and" because both are owed. Do not decide on the conjunction alone — ask whether one means substitutes for the other on a given occasion, or whether both are performed. If both are performed and each carries its own preparation, they are two duties however single the purpose they serve, and a statement naming both in a "through ..." or "by ..." clause is FUSED.
  · WHAT STILL COUNTS AS FUSED: parts that arise on SEPARATE OCCASIONS, from separate triggers, or as separate standing obligations the worker could be doing in any order and for unrelated reasons. Two record-keeping obligations owed to different bodies are two duties, and so is recording the thing you just did when the statement names both the doing and the recording.

THAT TAXONOMY GOVERNS WHICH DIFFERENCES MAY COUNT AS SEPARATE OUTCOMES HERE, and it is the same one the merge
and coverage judges are held to, so a verdict grounded outside it puts this check at odds with every step
downstream. Naming two outcomes is open to you only when they differ along a dimension that DIVIDES. Two parts
of a statement are therefore NOT two duties merely because they sit at different STAGES of the same matter
before the same counterpart -- seeking something and later reporting on it, opening a matter and closing it --
nor because the same act reaches its counterpart through a different MEDIUM, TOOL or SETTING. Where the
DOES-NOT list covers the only difference you can find, answer one_duty: true and say which bullet it was.
THE CUMULATIVE-MEANS RULE ABOVE SURVIVES THIS, BUT ONLY THROUGH THE ACT. Two means are two duties when they
are two different ACTS -- composing a document at a desk and conducting a session in person take different
steps, different judgement and different skill, which is why the surveyor's schedule and walkthrough divide.
They are NOT two duties when one act is merely carried over two media. Ask which it is before you split.

TESTS, IN ORDER:
  1. THE PLAN TEST. State the one-line plan the statement implies. If writing it needs an "or" across unrelated triggers, occasions, or purposes, the statement is fused.
  2. THE DIRECTION TEST. Ask who acts on whom, and to what end. Work the worker performs ON THEMSELVES OR THEIR OWN OBLIGATIONS is a different duty from work they perform ON OTHER PEOPLE, even when both are described with the same verb and both concern the same subject matter. Producing something is a different duty from reviewing or receiving it. THIS TEST DOES NOT REACH PRODUCING-AND-THEN-TRANSMITTING: the transaction exemption above settles those, and it comes first. Do not cite this test to split making a thing from lodging, sending, submitting or serving it.
  3. THE MEMBER-PARTITION TEST. Look at the activities filed under the statement. If they fall into groups that share no plan with each other — each group serving its own outcome — the statement is covering for a grouping that was never one duty. If instead every activity is a variant of the same action, it is one duty however varied the wording.
     A GROUP OF ONE IS A MISFILED ACTIVITY, NOT A FUSED DUTY. An earlier automated step did the filing and it is not perfect, so a long list routinely carries one stray that belongs elsewhere. One activity out of many, pointing somewhere the STATEMENT ITSELF does not go, is evidence about the CLUSTER and not about the sentence — the statement never claimed that work, so splitting it changes nothing the statement says. Require a real partition: each side needs several activities, or a single activity the statement actually names. Do not split a statement to accommodate one outlier.
  4. THE SUBSTITUTION TEST. Could a worker do everything in one part of the statement and nothing in another, and still be doing their job as the statement describes it? If yes, the parts are separate duties.
     INDEPENDENCE IS NOT FUSION, AND THIS TEST IS THE ONE MOST OFTEN MISREAD. Almost any statement naming two objects passes a bare "could you do one without the other" — that is true of every list ever written, and answering on it alone splits statements that are plainly one duty. The question is not whether the parts CAN occur apart; it is whether they SERVE UNRELATED ENDS. Ask what each part is FOR. If both feed the same outcome — the same case, the same delivery, the same obligation being discharged — they are one duty even when a worker could do one on a day they do not do the other. Only answer "fused" when doing one leaves the other's outcome untouched and unadvanced.

WHAT DOES NOT MATTER HERE: length, wording, how many nouns appear, whether the statement is broad or narrow, whether it duplicates some other task, and whether every activity is well covered. Judge ONLY whether it names one duty or several.

BE CONSERVATIVE. A statement that is merely long, or that lists many objects or examples, is ONE duty. Answer "fused" only when you can name the two or more separate outcomes and say which activities belong to each.

THE UNIT OF THE INVENTORY IS A DUTY A PRACTITIONER WOULD LIST, NOT AN ACT YOU CAN ARTICULATE. Almost every real duty decomposes into acts with their own objects and occasions, so being able to name two parts is not yet evidence of fusion. Before answering "fused", write each part as its own entry and ask whether it reads as a duty of the occupation someone could hold on its own. If a part reads instead as a chore of the same ongoing responsibility as the others — the recording that belongs to its accounts, the appraisal that belongs to its supervision, per the taxonomy's chores-of-one-responsibility bullet — the statement is ONE duty and the parts are its examples. This outranks "WHAT STILL COUNTS AS FUSED" above wherever the separate occasions are all the upkeep of one responsibility.

YOUR OUTCOMES ARE WORKING LABELS, NOT PUBLISHED PROSE. A split part that survives loses its statement and has its head REDRAWN by the writer from its members (apply_splits strips the stamps precisely so that happens), so do not labour the wording: one plain line per outcome, verb first, naming the act and its object clearly enough that a reader can tell the parts apart. What you MUST get exactly right is the MEMBERS — the partition is the evidence, the label is a sketch.
HOW A STATEMENT IS WRITTEN NEVER DECIDES THE VERDICT, and the distinction matters because this check gates every merge in the pipeline: a statement is NEVER fused because it uses house jargon, picks a weak verb, carries an empty purpose clause or omits a counterpart. Those are defects for another step to fix. If the only thing wrong with a statement is how it is written, answer one_duty: true.
